It had rained since the start in Como, and by the last climb there were eleven riders left in front. Matteo Valsecchi looked at none of them. He stood on the pedals at the steepest hairpin, rode away, and crossed the line in Bergamo forty two seconds clear with his arms still on the bars.

It is his first Monument, at 31, in the race he watched from the roadside as a boy. "I did not raise my arms because I did not believe it," he said. "I thought someone would come back."

## Six hours of water

The race never had a calm hour. The descents off the Ghisallo and the Sormano were slick enough that the favourites rode them with their hands on the brakes, and a crash on the way down to the lake took out four riders who had started the day as contenders.

Valsecchi's team kept him at the back of the front group for most of the afternoon. "We wanted him out of the wind and out of trouble," said his directeur sportif, Erik Lindqvist. "That was the whole plan. The rest was him."

> I had looked at that hairpin every day for a month. I knew if I went there, nobody would want to follow in the wet.

## The hairpin

The attack came eighteen kilometres from the finish, on the second to last bend of the final climb. Nobody reacted for almost ten seconds. By the time the chase organised itself, Valsecchi was out of sight in the mist.

Behind him, Jonas Aalberg and Pablo Ibarra traded turns all the way down the descent and into Bergamo's old town. They never saw him again until the podium.

## What happens next

Valsecchi's season ends here. He said he will spend the winter at home in Lecco, ride the same roads without a number on his back, and come back in March for Milano Sanremo.
